## What each document found

**Ready-made bots (RESOURCES_BOTS.md, finished, on main).**
No finished bot or strategy does what we want. Nothing verifiable plays
no-limit hold'em at every table size from 2 to 9, adapts to named opponents,
runs on this 16 GB laptop, and is ready in hours; each candidate covers two or
three of those. The famous research bots were never released. The nearest
multi-player strategy (NoRegrets) needs 36-50 GB of memory to train; this
laptop has 16. Every finished screen reader is Windows-only, and the one
advertised as Mac-native is an empty repository. Nothing surveyed plays 7, 8
or 9 seats with real bet sizing. One design does "play differently against
this named player" the way the operator wants (OpenHoldem), but it is
Windows-only. The one concrete action: measure against Slumbot, a free,
live, two-player opponent that answered from this laptop during the survey.
Do not buy PokerSnowie or PokerBotAI.

**Exploiting specific opponents (RESOURCES_EXPLOITATION.md, seven review
rounds, last fixes in, not yet landed).**
The bot needs three things: a scorecard machine that turns hand records into
per-player numbers (a "tracker"), a way to turn those numbers into a
different playing style, and records of real humans so the bot knows what
"normal" looks like before it has played anyone. Findings: a free Python
tracker (fpdb-3) has the best counting rules but cannot be copied in, only
rewritten. Two small research projects implement the exact method our design
chose. A free 2009 archive of 21.6 million real-money no-limit hands, with
anonymised player codes, is the right seed for the baseline, built one poker
site at a time. Commercial trackers need a separate "converter" for phone
poker apps, and the last two review rounds were spent getting those prices
exactly right: PokerTracker and Hold'em Manager sell the same converter at
$29-49 a month; DriveHUD sells its own at $29 a month; Hand2Note's phone-app
support only works on Windows. Nothing in that part should be bought until
the operator names the app they play on.

**Solvers and calculators (RESOURCES_SOLVERS.md, five review rounds, last
fixes in, not yet landed).**
A solver computes an unbeatable strategy for one situation. Findings from
real measurements on this Mac: heads-up (two-player) solving of a late-street
spot is free and takes under a second; a full three-street solve does not
finish in seven minutes, so only pruned trees are usable live. Hand ranking
and equity are a non-problem in C++ (hundreds of millions of hands a second),
though the fastest tool caps at six players. Multiway solvers for a Mac exist
but none can be driven by another program, so they are study tools, not
decision tools. Ready-made preflop charts for every table size cost $69-319
one-time. One open project (GTOpen) does per-opponent exploitation across
2-9 seats, but preflop only and with no licence file. The document
deliberately nominates no tool for the bot's decision path; that is the
reconciliation's call.

**Game engines (ENGINE_ALTERNATIVES.md, two review rounds, fixer stopped
mid-work, edits saved to its branch).**
The engine the project started on (fedden/poker_ai) cannot reach a real
52-card game inside the budget: at least 18 days of computing and 147 GB of
memory just to build its tables, and it has no idea of bet sizing at all.
Four alternatives run 52-card no-limit at 2-9 players today; OpenSpiel is
five and a half times faster than the next. A forty-line "decide during the
hand by playing it out at random" bot was built on OpenSpiel and beat five
random opponents by about +22 big blinds a hand over 3,467 hands, with no
training. That is a real result and a very low bar. With real bet sizing the
same approach gets too few play-outs to be better than noise, so the
practical shape is "think against a four-move menu, act with real sizing",
and the translation between the two has not been built. Training a strategy
in advance without simplification does not converge on this laptop; with
simplification it is untested, not ruled out.

## The decisions only the operator can make

Every survey stops at the same three doors and says so.

1. **The forefront rule.** The project rule says code we write must not
   choose a poker action. The engine survey's "decide during the hand"
   approach, the solvers survey's "rules driven by equity", and the bots
   survey's rule profiles all need a recorded exception to that rule, or must
   be refused. No document grants it.
2. **The engine.** Whether to abandon fedden/poker_ai for OpenSpiel. The
   engine survey recommends it unconditionally; the project's own plan still
   names the old engine as its basis.
3. **The opponent baseline.** Whether to seed "what a normal player looks
   like" from the outside 2009 archive (free, licensed for it), or have the
   bot sit and observe first, as the design prefers.

The four surveys were always meant to be read together in one
"reconciliation" step that answers these three. That step has not been
dispatched; it waits on the surveys landing.
